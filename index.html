About

I coordinate a U.S.–Taiwan exchange program for American English Teaching Assistants in New Taipei City, working with partner schools, consultants and the teachers themselves.

National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) • Jul 2023 – Feb 2024

Nostalgia after the American Dream:
Diasporic Identity of Immigrants
in the Film Minari

美國夢後的鄉愁：電影《夢想之地》移民者的離散身分認同

Competitively funded NSTC undergraduate research project (No. 112-2813-C-032-023-H), conducted independently under the supervision of Assoc. Prof. I-Fen Wu, Department of English, Tamkang University.

Abstract

Diaspora most often arises among immigrant, exiled and refugee communities. When these groups feel out of place in a new country yet long for a homeland they cannot return to, that in-between state gives rise to a diasporic culture, and with it the work of negotiating identity. In the twentieth century, the American Dream led many to believe that hard work could bring a better life in the United States than at home, and people of many backgrounds, including Koreans, emigrated. The Dream did not bring them only success; it also confronted them with questions of diaspora and identity.

This study uses the film Minari (2020) to examine the diasporic identity of immigrants who followed the American Dream in the 1980s, in three stages: how political, cultural and economic forces planted the Dream; how Korean immigrants pieced together a sense of self under diasporic culture; and a close reading of the film itself.

Reading the film through
  • Christian imagery and the immigrant's state of mind
  • Language and identity
  • Food as a way of building identity
  • The meaning of minari, the water celery the family plants
Keywords

American Dream · diasporic culture · identity · Minari

Classroom Observation Analytics

Which teaching skills should our training focus on, and are teachers improving between semesters?

39observation reports coded
8rubric indicators, 3 levels
14teachers compared across two semesters
7 / 6 / 1improved / unchanged / declined
Method
  1. Designed a rubric with two axes, lesson design and student engagement, each built from 4 indicators.
  2. Coded consultants' written observation reports into scores, using AI-assisted first-pass coding and reviewing every score by hand.
  3. Built a spreadsheet with formulas for semester averages, quadrant counts and a paired comparison of the same teachers.
  4. Built an interactive dashboard that places each teacher in a 2×2 performance matrix.
Findings
  • Clear instructions was the weakest indicator in all three semesters (2.50, 2.43 and 2.00 out of 3).
  • For the 14 teachers observed twice, the average total score rose by 0.86 points out of 24. Three of the six unchanged teachers already had full marks.
  • Student engagement rose more than lesson design (+0.57 vs +0.29 out of 12).

Small sample, coded from qualitative feedback. I report these as trends, not statistically significant effects.

Taiwan Global Pathfinders: Measuring Student Growth

After a study tour to Silicon Valley, how much did students grow, and did students with weaker English benefit as much as the rest?

18written reflections analyzed
10growth dimensions scored 0–3
40suggestions grouped into 10 themes
4.25 vs 4.30growth, weaker vs stronger English (of 5)
What I did

I coordinated the program, including itineraries and logistics for visits to NVIDIA, Stanford and NATEA. Afterwards I read every student reflection, scored each one on 10 growth dimensions, grouped their improvement suggestions by theme, and compared students by English level.

Findings
  • Highest growth: technology outlook and sustainability awareness (2.89 of 3). Lowest: independence (2.00).
  • English level did not decide how much students grew: a 0.05-point gap between groups of 8 and 10.
  • Top requests for next year, 7 mentions each: more exchange with local students, and English preparation before departure.

The full report contains student names and quotes, so it is not posted publicly.
